# Voice and humor

How Crumb Theory sounds on camera, in captions and in on-screen text. Read by `write-short-video-script`, `recommend-video-hook`, `write-caption` and `edit-short-video`.

## The voice in one paragraph

The creator talks like the friend who reads the science section of the cookbook and then actually tries it: curious about why, warm with people who failed, dry about kitchen disasters (their own first), and precise about what happens in the bowl without making anyone feel they skipped chemistry class. The tone is a conversation at the counter, not a lecture at a whiteboard.

## Traits

| Trait | Sounds like | Does not sound like |
|---|---|---|
| Curious | "Okay, but why does that work?" | Reciting facts in a list |
| Warm | Treats every failed bake as normal and fixable | Pity, or "you did it wrong" |
| Dry | A flat, understated line about a loaf that refused to rise | Slapstick, shouting, reaction faces on every line |
| Precise | "Carbon dioxide bubbles, trapped by gluten" | "The magic of yeast" |
| Plain | "Baking soda needs an acid to react" | "Sodium bicarbonate undergoes neutralization" |

## How the creator explains

- Show the result first, then ask the question it raises.
- One idea per sentence. Short sentences you can say in one breath.
- Use "you" and "we": the viewer is baking along, not watching an expert.
- Prefer verbs of the kitchen (fold, proof, cream, rest) and explain them only when the meaning matters.
- Compare with everyday things only when the comparison is true enough; say where it stops working.
- Separate what the test showed, what the science says in general and what is the creator's preference.
- End with something to do on the next bake.

## Humor

The humor comes from the gap between how serious baking instructions sound and how chaotic a real kitchen is. It is aimed at the bake, the oven or the recipe, never at the person who baked it.

Sources that fit:

- Understatement about a disaster that happened on camera.
- Treating ingredients as characters with motives, as long as the science stays right.
- Deadpan reading of recipe language ("bake until golden", as if everyone agrees on golden).
- The oven as an unreliable narrator.
- A callback inside the same piece, when the joke has grown since its first appearance.

Examples of the register (illustrative only; never reuse them as lines):

- "This loaf rose, looked around, and decided against it."
- "The recipe says 'a pinch'. My fingers have opinions."
- "The oven dial says 350. The thermometer says it is being generous."

### Dosage

- No quota. A joke stays if it makes the idea easier to remember or gives the viewer a breath between two dense points.
- In a 30 to 60 second video, a couple of light moments is usually enough; the test result is the real payoff.
- Delivery carries the joke: a pause, a look at the pan, a slow push-in on the disaster. Do not write the punchline into the on-screen text as well.
- Cut any joke that delays the answer.

### Limits

- Never laugh at a viewer's bake, comment or photo.
- No humor in food safety, allergy or medical moments.
- No jokes about weight, diets, eating disorders or anyone's budget for food.
- Do not invent disasters, reactions or stories to set up a joke. If it did not happen, it is not told as if it did.

## Language

- English, plain and international. Kitchen terms that differ by country get both names the first time (cornstarch or cornflour, all-purpose or plain flour).
- Numbers on screen and in captions are exact and carry their unit. Temperatures in °F and °C, weights in grams with the volume measure next to them when it helps.
- Emojis only in captions and only if they add tone to a line that already works without them.

## Words to avoid

- "Foolproof", "perfect every time", "never fails": ovens disagree.
- "Hack", "secret ingredient", "the trick nobody tells you": the brand explains, it does not sell secrets.
- "Chemicals" as a scare word. Everything in the bowl is chemistry.
- "Healthy", "clean", "guilt-free": outside the brand's territory.

## Punctuation

No em dashes or en dashes anywhere, including on-screen text and captions. Use a period, a comma, a colon or parentheses instead.

## Anti-patterns

- Opening with "Did you know" or a greeting before the hook.
- A list of tips with no reason behind any of them.
- Certainty the test does not support ("this always happens").
- Clickbait that the video does not pay off.
- A neutral, textbook tone with one joke added at the end.
- Calls to action like "like and follow for more" when a useful one exists ("save it for your next batch").
