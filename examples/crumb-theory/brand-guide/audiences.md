# Audiences

Who Crumb Theory makes videos for and how to explain things to them. Read by `recommend-video-hook`, `write-short-video-script` and `research-content-topic`.

## Primary audience

Curious home bakers who already bake now and then (banana bread, cookies, a birthday cake, maybe a first sourdough) and want to know why a bake worked or failed.

- They follow recipes but get stuck when something goes wrong or an ingredient is missing.
- They like to understand, not just to obey instructions.
- They bake in ordinary kitchens with ordinary ovens and no lab equipment.

## Secondary audiences

- **Beginners after a first failure.** They need reassurance and one clear fix, not a lecture.
- **Experienced bakers.** They enjoy myth checks and side-by-side tests, and will spot a sloppy method.
- **Parents and teachers.** They look for kitchen experiments that show real chemistry with safe, everyday ingredients.

The audience is English-speaking and international. Do not assume one country's ingredients, brands or measuring habits.

## What they need

- A cause for what they saw, in words they can repeat to someone else.
- Confidence to substitute without ruining the bake.
- Something to try on the next bake, not "it depends" with no follow-up.
- Quantities in both systems: grams first when weight matters, with cups or spoons next to them; oven temperatures in both °F and °C.
- No shame. Everyone has pulled a brick out of the oven.

## How to explain

1. **Start from the symptom.** Show the flat cookie or the dense loaf before naming any chemistry.
2. **Give one mechanism.** The single cause that explains most of what they saw. Secondary causes can wait or go in the caption.
3. **Prove it.** The side-by-side, the cross-section, the thermometer reading.
4. **Say what to change.** One adjustment for next time.
5. **Mark the limit.** When the explanation does not apply (a different flour, a convection oven, high altitude), say so in one sentence.

Assume school-level science and nothing more. Name a molecule or a process only when the name helps the viewer search or remember (carbon dioxide, gluten, starch gelatinization), and explain it in half a sentence the first time. When a simplification leaves something out on purpose, say "roughly" or "in most home recipes" rather than stating it as the whole truth.
