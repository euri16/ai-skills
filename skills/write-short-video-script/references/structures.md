# Short script structures

Sources: [MrBeast's production memo](https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/memo-how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/) (2024, transcription), Parker and Stone's "but / therefore" rule (reported by [The Hustle](https://thehustle.co/the-storytelling-secrets-that-netted-matt-stone-and-trey-parker-600-million-dollars)), [Dan Harmon's story circle](https://industrialscripts.com/dan-harmons-story-circle/amp/), [TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices?lang=en) and, as reported and without links, Jenny Hoyos's method, the PAS copywriting formula and improv teaching (UCB, Second City). Links checked on 2026-11-20.

## Common anatomy

Every serious source describes the same backbone, at a different scale:

| Part | Function | In a 60 s Reel |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | Open promise of the payoff, readable in silence | 0 to 3 s |
| Promise or preview | What they will understand or see; in narrative, the conflict to come | 3 to 8 s |
| Beats | Two or three units of new information, each one a "but" or "therefore" relative to the previous one, with a concrete example | 8 to 52 s |
| Re-hook | New question, twist, example, change of register, demo; restate the premise halfway | About every 15 to 20 s (see below) |
| Payoff | Delivers what the hook promised | The 3 to 5 s before the CTA |
| CTA | By default one sentence, right after the payoff; the video ends with it | Last 2 to 3 s |
| Cut | Hard, no goodbye or outro | Last frame |

Parker and Stone's rule decides the order of the beats: if "and then" fits between two beats, there is no story. If "but" (obstacle, contradiction, nuance) or "therefore" (consequence) fits, there is causality and the person needs the next beat to close the previous one.

**Re-hook cadence.** MrBeast's memo plans a "re-engagement" around minute 3 and another around minute 6 of a long video. Scaled to a 60 second piece, that gives a re-hook about every 15 to 20 seconds: two or three in a minute. It is an inference used as a starting recommendation, not data. Place the re-hooks where the account's retention curves show people leaving, and never add one that brings no new information.

## Variants by format

### Educational talking head

To explain an idea, a mental model or a decision.

1. Hook: result first, direct promise, common mistake or real question.
2. Promise: "in 40 seconds you'll understand why X and what to do".
3. Beat 1: the situation that seems simple, with an example.
4. Beat 2 ("but"): the real condition that complicates it, visible on screen.
5. Beat 3 ("therefore"): the mental model or the solution, shown while it is explained.
6. Nuance: the limit or the case where it does not apply, in one sentence.
7. Payoff: the reusable rule or the experiment the person can do today.

A move from long-form explanatory writing that fits compressed: simple situation, real condition, consequence, why the obvious options are not enough, solution, rule. The six steps do not fit in 60 seconds; choose three.

### Tool or product demo

PAS structure (pain, agitate, solve) without sounding like a sale.

1. Hook: the result working at second 0, or before and after in split screen.
2. Pain: the concrete friction the tool removes, in a sentence the audience recognizes.
3. Demo: show the main path while narrating; each step on screen is a beat. Only what is needed to understand; not a full tutorial.
4. Limit: what it does not do, what it cost, when it is not a good fit. Saying what something cannot do is what gives the demo credibility.
5. Payoff: who it is for and what to do first.

Opening with a logo, title card or the face without context is forbidden. Editorial independence: compare by problem, not by affiliation; disclose commercial relationships.

### Opinion

Structure of a conflict of ideas.

1. Hook: contrarian with conviction, real story or specific question.
2. Acknowledge why the common belief seems reasonable, in one fair sentence.
3. Beat ("but"): the evidence or experience that forces a revision of it.
4. Beat ("therefore"): the creator's stance, with its condition.
5. Payoff: what they would do differently and when they would change their mind.

Separate fact, inference and opinion from the second sentence on. No provoking for the sake of provoking.

### Build in public

Harmon's story circle compressed into four beats.

1. Situation: the project and its real state, with a verifiable figure if one exists.
2. Need: the problem or the decision at hand.
3. Trial with a cost: what was tried, what broke, what it cost (time, money, pride).
4. Return changed: what was learned and what comes next.

In 45 seconds or less, merge situation and need into a single beat. Natural hooks: experiment or Day N, real figure, in medias res with the work already on screen. Every data point must be real; never invent results. Protect confidential information of clients, employers and third parties.

### Humor sketch

Classic structure compressed (UCB, Second City, Kyle Gordon).

1. Base reality in 1 to 2 seconds: where we are, who is normal.
2. First unusual thing, framed immediately with the on-screen headline ("When...", "POV:", "Monday me:") or with another character's reaction.
3. The "game": the unspoken rule. "If this is true, what else is true."
4. Three steps that escalate with one of these levers: specifics (more precise details, not always bigger), emotion (how much the character cares), stakes (how much the world cares). The third breaks the pattern.
5. Button: a line or image that reinterprets the connector (the mug, the alarm, the to-do list) and closes. Hard cut.

Each line is a joke or a step; cut all the exposition. Everyday, recognizable premise (affiliative humor) over the surreal. A character with full confidence and no self-awareness. Timing: a short pause before the strong punchline as a tool to test, a quick cut to the reaction, a reaction zoom, holding a shot one second longer when the silence is the joke. For an international audience: keep the lines in the creator's own voice, as the voice guide describes it; adapt only very local institutional references; universal premise (being late, pride, the group chat) with local detail as texture. An optional humor device, for creators whose voice guide welcomes it: a short ironic on-screen label on a funny detail the footage itself offers.

### Visual video

The creator is not on camera: motion graphics scenes, with screenshots, b-roll or generated images when the piece has them, tell the piece in 15 to 45 seconds, with the creator's voice-over or without voice. The backbone is the common anatomy; what changes is who carries each part.

- When the creator narrates, the narration carries the story and each scene shows the thing being named. On-screen text is the subtitles plus what the voice does not say (a figure, a date, a label).
- With no narration, the script is what gets written on screen: one idea per screen, a headline of up to 8 words and at most one supporting line, written to be read once (budget in `pacing.md`). The music marks the rhythm.
- Every beat is a scene with one lead visual that does something: a figure that counts, a document with its key line marked, a label that gets struck out, steps that get their check. A beat with nothing to show is cut or merged.
- The hook is frame 0: the headline and its image are understood in silence in about a second. No title card.

An announcement or a finding, the most common case:

1. Hook: the news or the result in one headline over the thing itself (the product, the figure, what changed).
2. Proof: where it comes from, with the source and its date on screen long enough to read.
3. Beat ("but" or "therefore"): what it changes for this audience, with one concrete case.
4. Stance: what the creator would do with it, or what is still unknown. Without a stance the piece is only a headline.
5. Payoff and CTA on the closing screen.

Other shapes that fit the format: a comparison (the two things side by side, then the verdict), steps (one screen per step, arriving from the side), a single figure explained (the number, what it measures, what it means for the viewer).

## Re-hooks that work

- A new question the previous beat opened.
- A concrete example after an abstract idea.
- A twist: "but this breaks when...".
- Change of register: from explanation to mental image, from serious to an observation with humor.
- Going from camera to screen, or from screen to camera.
- Restating the premise: "remember, the goal was...".
- A real figure or result on screen.

Spoken connectors that announce the twist and are the out-loud version of "but" and "therefore": "but there's a problem", "and here's the important part", "watch out for this", "let me explain why", "the part almost nobody checks". Use one per re-hook, not in every sentence; some retention guides ask for a change every 3 to 5 seconds (for example [Socialync](https://www.socialync.io/blog/retention-techniques-keep-viewers-watching-2026), a tool vendor's blog), which in a talking head sounds like a salesperson.

What does not re-hook: a cut without new content, a zoom, a sound effect, another joke right after the previous one.

## CTAs

- **Position:** by default, one sentence right after the payoff, and the video ends there (a brand guide that closes its videos another way wins). A CTA before the payoff interrupts the promise the person stayed for; anything after the CTA is an outro.
- **A single CTA.** A chained "Like, share and follow" is an anti-pattern.
- **Which one.** Instagram names watch time, likes and sends as its main signals, with sends weighing somewhat more with non-followers. No platform data shows that asking for a send produces more sends, so a send CTA ("send it to whoever is planning this", "pass it to your team") is a recommendation for pieces someone would really forward, not a lever on reach.
- Saves come from steps, templates, checklists and examples; shares, from identifiable opinions and surprising results (editorial observation, no study).
- Asking to "comment a word, number or emoji" counts as engagement bait on Instagram. An open question the creator wants to answer is fine.

## Mistakes that bore

| Mistake | Why |
|---|---|
| Greeting, intro or long context before the hook | The proposition must be understood in 3 seconds; context goes after the promise, and a series intro, if its plan defines one, right after the hook |
| Abstraction without an example | Showing while explaining retains more than explaining and then showing |
| Single shot and monotone voice for more than 30 to 40 seconds | A drop-off risk; alternate camera and material with a purpose |
| "And then, and then" | Without causality there is no reason to stay |
| Not keeping the hook's promise | Sinks the second half and erodes trust |
| Signaling the end ("and that's it", logo, goodbye) | People leave as soon as the ending is announced; go from the payoff to the one-sentence CTA and cut |
| Having no stance | Shares come from identifiable opinions |
| Long lists or several jokes in a row | Stacking non-progressive content tires; one playful moment at a time |
| Over-editing to cover a weak script | "Fast cuts and lots of graphics won't fix a bad story" (`pacing.md`) |
| Bad audio | Immediate cause of drop-off and loss of credibility, especially on LinkedIn |
