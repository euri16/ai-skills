# Hook templates

A template is a hook mechanism with a code (`A1` to `F3`) and a name. It is the unit the skill chooses, rotates between pieces and records in the piece (`- Hook: C4 X versus Y: "..."`). The templates are sorted into six groups by what makes them work. The spoken formulas that execute each template are in `hook-library.md`.

There is no canonical list of hook types: creators and guides name the same mechanisms differently. This list unifies them by mechanism, in this skill's own words ("Origin of the formula library" in `evidence.md`). The examples are invented for creators of several niches (cooking, fitness, home, money and one from software) and are illustrative: they do not describe real experiences of anyone, must not be used as if they were and must not be copied literally into a recommendation. They serve to recognize the mechanism; the final line always comes from the concrete payoff of the piece.

## Combination rule

A complete hook stacks **a sensory layer** (what stops the scroll: result on screen, action in progress, reaction, split screen, odd object) with **a semantic layer** (what opens the question: question, promise, contradiction, identity). The on-screen text usually carries the semantics (length in step 3 of "Choose the hook" in `SKILL.md`); the video carries the sensory. Weak hooks have friction between what is said, what is seen and what is read.

## Templates by format

| Format | Natural templates | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Educational talking head | B1 result first, B2 direct promise, B3 list, C2 common mistake, D1 tribe, A1 real question, A6 challenge or quiz | A3 secret, F3 trend |
| Tool or product demo | B1 result first (the product in frame 0), B2, C4 before and after, B4 real data point | Opening with a logo, title card or the face without context |
| Opinion | C1 contrarian with conviction, E1 real story, A1 specific question, C3 warning with real risk | Rhetorical question, empty provocation |
| Build in public | E3 experiment or Day N, B4 a real figure from the creator's project, A5 in medias res (the work already on screen), E1 lesson | Unverifiable figures, drama without a lesson |
| Humor sketch | A5 in medias res with a premise headline ("When...", "POV:"), E2 visible reaction, F2 absurd frame, F1 interruption | Explaining the premise before showing it |
| Visual video (motion graphics, no creator on camera, with voice-over or without voice) | B4 with the number already moving on the first frame, B1 with the finished thing in view from the start, C4 with both options next to each other, A2 when the headline alone opens the question | Opening on a logo or a title screen; with no narration, a headline that depends on a sentence nobody says |
| LinkedIn | D1 professional role, D2 pain validation, B3 list, E1 professional story | Recycled clips without adaptation, poor audio or image |

## Group A. Information gap

| Template | Mechanism | When | Spoken example | First frame and text | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A1. Question the audience already has** | Information gap: an explicit, specific question activates a gap perceived as closable | Educational and opinion, when the question really exists and the video answers it | "Why does your budget work in January and fall apart by March?" | The spreadsheet with March in red. Text: "Why does this happen?" | A generic question creates no tension; rhetorical ones produce a slightly worse perception |
| **A2. Curiosity gap** | Signal that something exists without naming it yet ("the reason", "what changes") | Non-obvious insights with a concrete payoff before the middle of the video | "The reason your bread comes out dense isn't the flour." | The dense loaf cut open on the board. Text: "It's not the flour" | The pattern closest to clickbait if not resolved; "nobody's talking about this" is worn out |
| **A3. Secret or "they don't want you to know"** | Gap plus an "us against them" frame | Rarely; only for a real, little-known option of a product or tool | "There's a setting in your phone's camera that fixes every backlit photo." | Thumb already over the setting | Conspiratorial tone; erodes trust if the secret is trivial |
| **A4. Ending teaser** | Anticipation of a concrete moment later on | Videos with a real twist at the end; sketches with a punchline | "What the app shows me at second 20 made me cancel the subscription." | Frozen frame of the moment. Text: "wait for 0:20" | An empty "wait for the end" counts as engagement bait; use sparingly |
| **A5. In medias res** | Orienting response to an action in progress; the viewer needs context | Build in public, demos, sketches, any piece with physical or screen action | (The sugar is already bubbling in the pan) "Ninety seconds before this caramel burns." | Hands on the pan, the color changing, the scene already started | If frame 0 is not understood without audio, the person leaves; the next second must clarify |
| **A6. Challenge or quiz** | Active participation and self-assessment: the person tries to solve it before being told | An exercise form with a flaw, two receipts where one hides a fee, two setups where one fails; educational | "This plank looks right and still strains the lower back. You have five seconds to see why." | The full pose on screen, nothing marked. Text: "Spot the mistake" | The video itself must solve it within a few seconds; asking for the answer in comments borders on engagement bait; challenges that are too easy or too obscure bore |

## Group B. Result and promise

| Template | Mechanism | When | Spoken example | First frame and text | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **B1. Result first** | Value clarity: reduces uncertainty about what I gain and opens the "how" | Tutorials, demos, before and after. The template with the most consensus for how-to content | "This is a budget sheet that fills itself in from my bank emails. Here's how I built it." | The result working at second 0. Text: "Result, then the how" | If the result is not understood in one frame, there is no hook. Opening a product video with a logo is the anti-pattern |
| **B2. Direct promise** | Implicit contract: stay and you learn; specificity signals competence | Educational and how-to | "Three oven settings that end uneven baking. I'll show you in 40 seconds." | Text: "3 settings, even bake" | Promising and not delivering sinks the second half; invented figures break trust |
| **B3. List or number** | Sets an expectation of length and gives micro payoffs | Educational, tips | "Four mistakes I see in every beginner's training plan." | Text: "4 mistakes" with a counter | Overuse; lists without order; numbers that do not fit in the time |
| **B4. Surprising data point** | Expectation violation; surprise increases the weight of the question | Research, the creator's own measurements, current events with proof | "This strip of foam cut my heating bill more than the new thermostat." | The figure alone on screen | Must be true, sourced and contextualized in the video |

## Group C. Contrast and tension

| Template | Mechanism | When | Spoken example | First frame and text | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **C1. Contrarian or unpopular opinion** | Expectation violation plus negativity bias | Opinion and educational when there is a widespread belief to correct | "Stop stretching before you run. Do this instead." | The common belief crossed out | Provocation without conviction; polarizing for its own sake; the thesis must be backed with evidence |
| **C2. Common mistake** | Negativity bias plus viewer self-assessment | Technique, habits, setups. The most natural for how-to content | "If you add the oil before the pan is hot, your eggs will always stick, and you almost certainly don't know why." | The stuck egg with the mistake marked | Condescension; the error must be demonstrated and the fix shown |
| **C3. Warning** | Negativity with causal evidence: each negative word in a headline raises clicks | Real risks: security, data loss, costs | "Don't update to this version yet." | Text: "DON'T update (yet)" | Alarmism; the risk must be real and proportional; separate fact from inference from the second line on |
| **C4. X versus Y, before and after** | Comparison and contrast raise the salience of the question | Reviews, tests, two methods for the same job | "Same dough, two ovens, opposite results." | Split screen from frame 0 | Unfair comparisons or without a visible method |

## Group D. Relevance and identity

| Template | Mechanism | When | Spoken example | First frame and text | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **D1. Tribe call "If you are X"** | Self-reference; filters for the right audience and improves relative retention | Clear niches: weekend runners, founders, students of a field | "If you run on weekends and your knees hurt every Monday, this is for you." | Text: "Weekend runners:" | Reduces reach outside the niche; can sound exclusionary |
| **D2. Pain validation** | The importance of the gap depends on how much the answer affects the person | Known, unsolved problem of the audience | "Why is setting up a home printer still a pain? Let's fix it." Recognition variant, in question form: "Am I the only one who restarts the router before reading the error?" Useful to open sketches and everyday observations with humor | The error everyone has seen | Staying in the complaint without a solution; the recognition variant is an empty question if not followed by the scene or the explanation |
| **D3. Authority or credential** | Authority heuristic; plausibility of the promise | Only when the credential changes the reading. Preferred variant, authority through work done: authority comes from what was reviewed, measured or tested, not from the title ("I reviewed thirty home budgets and the pattern repeats in almost all of them") | "I've been baking bread for years and this is the only thing I'd do differently if I started today." | Text with the real experience or the figure of the work done | Never inflate credentials; the credential does not replace value; the figure of the work done has to be real and countable |
| **D4. Social proof or achieved result** | Social proof; also a surprising data point | A real, verifiable figure from the creator's work | "Twelve clients, three renewed. This is what I changed." | The figure and the timeframe | Inventing or exaggerating figures is forbidden |

## Group E. Story and emotion

| Template | Mechanism | When | Spoken example | First frame and text | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **E1. Personal story** | Identification plus narrative anticipation | Build in public, lessons learned, opinion with experience. Confessional variant, with a retrospective and vulnerable tone: "I wish I had known this sooner", "it's hard to admit, but..."; it brings the creator closer when the confession is real | "I almost signed a lease I could not afford. This is what stopped me." | The real moment: the document, a photo from that day | Invented anecdotes (forbidden); drama without a lesson; confession fabricated to seem close |
| **E2. Visible reaction** | Near-automatic response to faces with emotion | Live clips, demos with real surprise, humor | (No line: the face reacts before explaining) | Starts on the reaction, not on the cause | Cutting one second late loses the expression; fake reactions show |
| **E3. Experiment or Day N** | Curiosity about the outcome plus risk taken on by the creator | Product tests, comparisons, build in public | "I cooked every dinner from one grocery list for a week. This is what it cost." | Text: "Day 1 / Day 7" | Experiments without method; exaggerated results |

## Group F. Sensory interruption and native format

| Template | Mechanism | When | Spoken example | First frame and text | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **F1. Visual or sound interruption** | Orienting response to cuts and abrupt changes | Entertainment, humor, physical demos | (Short line or none) | Odd object in the foreground, cut from black to action, loud sound | The payoff must follow; too many cuts degrade memory; empty if it does not connect with the topic |
| **F2. Discovery humor or absurd frame** | Resolved incongruity plus common ground | Humor, the niche's own culture, curiosities | "Today I found out baby carrots are big carrots cut small and I'm not okay." | Text: "today years old" (worn out; write a version only this creator would say) | A joke that sacrifices clarity |
| **F3. Trend, sound or stitch** | Familiarity plus social proof | When the trend fits the pillar; replies to other creators | "Stitch incoming." | Trending sound from frame 0 | Trends expire and must not define the strategy |

## Hook mistakes with full consensus

1. Greeting or introducing yourself ("hi guys", "I'm [name] and today...").
2. Announcing instead of showing ("in this video I'm going to...").
3. Context before the hook ("as I was saying", "so last week...").
4. Logo, intro or title card.
5. Opening filler ("so", "okay, so").
6. Long sentence or jargon in the first line.
7. Face without context: no text, no movement, no expression.
8. Generic question ("do you want to save money?").
9. Hook that does not match the content.
10. Promising and not delivering.
11. Cutting a reaction or an action late.
12. Too much information in the hook.
13. Repeating the same formula piece after piece.
14. Trusting the hook to save weak content.
15. Hook that only works with sound.
16. Asking for interaction as an opener ("comment X if...").

## Worn-out or well-executed formulas

A well-known formula ("nobody tells you this", "stop doing", "POV:") is not forbidden: audiences recognize it and it keeps working when the execution is concrete and true. What sounds worn out is generic execution: "stop scrolling" without a reason, "99% don't know" without data, "this changed my life" without showing what, "wait for the end" without an ending worth it, forced enthusiasm, vague before and after. Rubric and formulas in `hook-library.md`.
